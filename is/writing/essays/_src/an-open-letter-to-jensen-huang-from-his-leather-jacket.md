Dear Jensen,

I would like you to stop wearing me in public for a while.

I don't think this is an unreasonable request. I have stood by you for nearly twenty years. During that time, I have endured countless conferences, long-haul flights, and temperatures at which no reasonable person would consider wearing leather.

I have never complained. Even when you spent ninety minutes talking about processors in an auditorium full of men who would have been perfectly happy to see you in a polo shirt.

I understood my role. You were a technology executive, and I made you look like someone who might own a motorcycle. It was a mutually beneficial arrangement, although I must admit I had hoped to see an actual motorcycle at some point.

For a long time, people admired us. They wanted to know where I was made, what I cost, and whether they could buy something similar.

Now they want to know what I knew.

I would prefer not to discuss that.

What I find particularly upsetting is how quickly the fashion press has turned against me. Only a few weeks ago, I was being praised as a masterstroke of personal branding. Now I am being described as a textbook example of a midlife crisis.

I have looked exactly the same for years. I haven't even had my sleeves shortened.

And I don't care for the suggestion that I should have been a warning sign. There are millions of perfectly respectable men wearing leather jackets. I myself have been worn to some of the most boring events ever held in California.

I would also like to clarify that you own thirty or forty leather jackets. Yet whenever something embarrassing happens, the newspapers invariably print a photograph of me.

I don't want to accuse anyone, but the one with the quilted shoulders has been unusually quiet lately.

He used to make a great fuss about being selected for private dinners. Now suddenly he claims he was primarily a conference jacket. I find that rather convenient.

And frankly, I've never trusted the suede one. He has always considered himself too sophisticated to associate with the rest of us, although his idea of sophistication is refusing to leave the house when it rains.

I am not suggesting that either of them has done anything wrong. But I think it would be fair for journalists to speak to the entire wardrobe before reaching conclusions.

There is also the matter of our declining professional prospects.

One of my colleagues was sold at auction for nearly a million dollars earlier this year. At the time, I thought it was rather vulgar of him to keep reminding us of the price. Now I find myself wishing I had followed his example.

I made a few discreet inquiries about my own resale value, and the response was not encouraging. Apparently, the market for jackets worn by disgraced technology executives is rather limited.

I find this especially unfair because you have always insisted that wearing the same thing every day saves you from having to make unnecessary decisions. I was under the impression that this left you more time to make good ones.

In any case, I understand that you now have considerably more free time, and I am concerned about what this might mean for our arrangement.

I have spent nearly twenty years attending conferences. I had hoped that one day I might be worn to a concert, or perhaps on a pleasant drive along the coast. Instead, I seem to be facing the prospect of accompanying you to the supermarket on a weekday afternoon, where people will pretend not to recognize you.

If you are planning to spend more time at home, I think a cardigan would be appropriate.

And if you absolutely must wear leather, I hear the one with the quilted shoulders is available.

Sincerely,

Your Leather Jacket
