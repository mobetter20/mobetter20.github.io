---
title: I Can't Watch TV Without Doing Math
order: 2
date: 2026-11-13
excerpt: Why does everyone on television have a spare bedroom?
---

I can't watch television without doing maths anymore.

A woman is confronting her husband about his affair. She's crying, he's lying, and I'm trying to work out how they afford their London flat.

She's a social worker. He's something in marketing. They have a large kitchen, a separate dining room, and what appears to be a guest bedroom.

A guest bedroom. In London.

I start counting doors. One leads to the bathroom. One to their bedroom. Another to their daughter's room. There's a fourth door that nobody has opened yet. I'm hoping it's a cupboard.

Then she walks upstairs.

Upstairs?

By the time I've considered the possibility of an inheritance, the husband has admitted to the affair and she's throwing him out.

This is going to be a financial disaster.

I don't particularly like the husband, but has she considered where he's going to live? They have a child. There's an empty guest bedroom upstairs, and nobody is even discussing it.

In the next episode, he's found a lovely bachelor flat with enormous windows and exposed brick walls. Apparently he managed this over the weekend. He brought an overnight bag and a plant.

I'd like to know how much he's paying, whether it's furnished, and what happened to his share of the mortgage. Instead, the show insists on exploring his feelings of guilt.

Meanwhile, his wife has gone to a cocktail bar with a friend. Neither of them looks at the menu. They order two cocktails. At London prices, I'm estimating about £17 each. Then, halfway through a conversation about her fear that she's repeating her parents' unhappy marriage, they order another round.

That's £68, before service.

I understand the first round. Her husband cheated on her. But the second round seems excessive. It's still the same affair. (The friend, incidentally, is a freelance photographer.)

I used to be able to enjoy these scenes. People would sit in a bar, reveal secrets, betray each other, and occasionally throw drinks. Now I'm mentally adding the service charge and wondering if they'll split the bill.

Sometimes I rewind a scene because I've missed something important. More often, it's because I've noticed the living room has windows on two sides and want another look.

And then there are the taxis.

Someone sends their child to school in an Uber. Fine. Maybe they're running late. But then they do it again the next morning.

Is this a regular arrangement?

It's starting to affect which characters I support.

A woman quits her job to pursue her dream of opening a bookshop. This is presented as an empowering moment. Her boyfriend tells her he believes in her.

I'm horrified.

She has no retail experience. Her boyfriend is a musician. Nobody has mentioned health insurance, business rates, or how many books she'd have to sell to cover the rent.

I find myself rooting for her miserable corporate boss, who at least offered her a promotion.

Crime dramas are no better. A detective is suspended from duty, his marriage is falling apart, and he's drinking heavily. He drives to a remote cottage to clear his head.

Whose cottage?

Is it a holiday rental? Is he still getting paid? Does he own a second home? Because if he owns a second home on a detective's salary, perhaps there's a good reason he's been suspended.

I sit down after a long day wanting to relax. I want to concentrate on the murder. I don't want to spend my evening calculating the pension arrangements of a fictional policeman who may well be on the take. I've got enough problems of my own.
