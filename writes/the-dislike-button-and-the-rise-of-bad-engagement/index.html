A good time to be Randall Flagg.

“There’s no such thing as bad publicity” has become a business model on social media. For people who live off outrage, there’s no such thing as bad engagement. It feels like a good time to be Randall Flagg.

In 2021, YouTube hid public dislike counts. The button stayed, but viewers could no longer see how many other people disapproved. The count was open to abuse. It also did something useful: it helped separate attention from approval.

A 2026 study of diabetes-related YouTube videos found more comments, longer comments and more negative sentiment after the counts were hidden. Making disapproval less visible had apparently not made the conversation any friendlier.

We watch because we are angry, comment to condemn the behavior and share it because other people need to see how bad it is. We give the people we condemn a bigger stage.

And sometimes that person runs for office. In Japan, Hezuma Ryu filmed himself harassing a Black shopkeeper for views. He later won a seat on Nara’s city council, campaigning to protect deer and residents “from foreigners.” Someone known for causing trouble was now offering to protect people from it.

And what are supporters being offered? Sometimes, the expression of their anger passes for an answer to it. Someone names an enemy, stages a confrontation and makes the people they dislike furious. It feels like something has been accomplished.

The feedback loop deforms. Disapproval becomes publicity. Other people’s anger becomes evidence that you are doing something right. There is always another confrontation to stage while the original problem remains.

The Walking Dudes are here. They might self-destruct, but they can take a lot of people with them.
